---

«ВАНЯДУМ» holds **one заброшка for the whole process**. Everybody is in it, opening a socket is walking in, the pickups come back, and nothing ever ends. It replaces one arena per run — the half of [ADR-048](./ADR-048-the-simulation-is-a-server-owned-fixed-step.md) reversed on the owner's decision of 2026-08-02 — and the durable row that used to record a finished run now records a **visit**.

_Why one world is not re-argued here._ [ADR-056](./ADR-056-the-office-is-one-process-wide-arena-not-one.md) made that case for the sibling office and it holds unchanged: a private world cannot express the only behaviour worth having several people for, and converting to a shared one is a rewrite of the state model, the tick, the snapshot addressing and the capacity rule arriving under the name of a feature. That record named this game as its counter-example, on the grounds that a run is a freshly generated building nobody else asked for. That was true of a game with an objective and it stopped being true when the objective went. **What this record has to add is the part the office never had to decide: this game's world is GENERATED.** The office is static and lives in the catalogue, so it is the same office for ever and has nothing to say about its own identity. A заброшка is a pure function of a seed, sent whole over HTTP once ([ADR-050](./ADR-050-the-level-is-generated-on-the-server-and-sent.md)) — so sharing one raises two questions the office cannot: *which* building is this, and what becomes of it when everybody leaves.

_Regeneration is a lifecycle, not a schedule._ The building is torn down by the tick that removes the **last** occupant, and the next arrival generates a fresh seed. There is no timer, no rotation and no vote, and three properties fall out of that which are the whole reason for choosing it:

- **Nothing regenerates under anybody's feet**, because at the instant it happens there is nobody standing in it.
- **A level is never re-sent mid-session.** ADR-050's "generated once and sent once" survives exactly — the expensive alternative, pushing a few kilobytes of geometry to every connected client and having them rebuild every mesh, is not a thing this design can be asked to do.
- **The world is well-defined at every instant**, which is what makes it testable: there is no state in which the building exists but is being replaced.

**`world_id` is what makes it safe**, and it is the only invalidation signal there is. The client caches the level it fetched with the id it came with; the `vanyadum_ready` frame names the building it has just been let into; a mismatch means the geometry is of a заброшка that no longer exists, and the client re-fetches. Thirty-six characters, once per attach — nothing on a repeating frame ([ADR-037](./ADR-037-one-account-is-one-entity-and-the-wire.md) is the same reasoning applied to a face). The same frame also names the reader's own **slot**, for the same reason and at the same cost: it cannot change while he is in the building, and nothing else would ever tell him, because a snapshot names everybody except its own reader. **And it now names a third thing on exactly that argument, which is the point at which the argument is worth stating as a rule.** `Ready.Seq` is how far the world has already counted this occupant's commands — `Occupant.highSeq`, the mark `Enqueue` deduplicates against — so a predictor built at this attach resumes at `Seq + 1`. It is needed because an occupant **outlives its socket** for `AbandonGrace`: a page reload, a tab whose socket dropped, or a заброшка regenerated underneath a client all hand `createPredictor` a counter starting at one under a world still counting in the hundreds, and every command sent until the first snapshot lands is then dropped in silence — 4 of them at a 60 ms round trip, 14 at 300 ms, and a trigger pull is as droppable as a step. About ten bytes per socket, omitted at zero for everybody walking in for the first time, against the 120 B/s the same six-byte field would cost on the snapshot at 20 Hz — nearly four times the whole of what is left. So the three fields are one rule rather than three concessions: **what is constant for the life of an attachment is published once at the attachment, and a resume hint belongs on the frame sent when there is something to resume.**

One asymmetry in that lifecycle is deliberate and easy to get wrong. **A world nobody has joined yet is neither advanced nor torn down.** Not advanced, because there is nobody to simulate for or to tell; not torn down, because "generated and empty" is the ordinary state between a client fetching the level over HTTP and its socket saying hello a moment later. Destroying it in that gap would hand that client geometry whose id can never match — for ever, since every retry loses the same race.

_Joining is opening the socket, because the room already knows who you are._ There is no join endpoint, no lobby and no code. The account is bound at the WebSocket upgrade and travels to the game as a `realtime.Member` ([ADR-033](./ADR-033-a-game-reads-the-socket-through-a-game.md)), so being in the room *is* being in the building and the `vanyadum_hello` frame is the whole of the join. What a `POST /join` would buy is a **second door**, and two doors have to be kept agreeing about who is inside — the failure being a request that reserved a place for a socket that never opened, which is exactly the shape of the run-in-progress conflict this game used to serve a 409 for. Removing the door removes the state with it: no run id, no resume path, no "you are already playing", no «сдаться» button. Leaving is closing the tab.

That has one cost and it is stated rather than discovered: **a hello is a visit**, with no minimum length, so a socket that opens and immediately dies writes a zero-second row. The answer is a contract and not a constant — *the client sends `hello` when the player enters the building, not when the splash screen loads.* A `MinVisitSeconds` floor of the kind the office carries is bought against a defect nobody has seen here, and it earns its place there because a shift is a score somebody could pollute a leaderboard with, where a visit is not scored at all.

_A refusal is answered rather than swallowed._ Silence is this game's policy for a frame it **cannot parse** — a reply or a log line per bad frame, at the ten a second the socket permits, is a flood lever handed to any client. A hello the server parsed perfectly and cannot honour is a different thing, so a full building replies with a `vanyadum_full` frame and the client says so in Russian. Three details are deliberate: it is on the **socket** rather than on `GET /world`, because the socket is the only door and a second refusal would be two paths to one outcome — and an HTTP refusal could not reserve the place anyway, since nothing would hold it between the request and the upgrade; it carries **no fields**, because the capacity is already in the catalogue the client fetched once; and it names **no time to try again**, because nothing here ends and there is no honest one to give.

_Capacity is derived rather than chosen, and what derives it is everything a viewer is sent._ **The rule is the decision; the digit is only ever its current answer.** The building's population is whatever fits under **8 kB/s** to one viewer — the ceiling this game's design named as the point at which interest management stops being optional. **Three** terms are counted against it *together*, because a phone does not care which frame the bytes belonged to: the **snapshot**, built per occupant at the simulation rate and carrying everybody else *plus* every нейрослоп that reader can see; the **standings**, once a second, carrying everybody including the reader; and the **events**, priced at their own rate rather than at the snapshot's, since one is delivered once and cleared on the next frame, and bounded by the densest heap a building can hold coming back once per `PickupRespawn`. All three are measured at the widest quantisation the wire can carry rather than at the magnitudes a заброшка of a few tens of metres actually produces, because a phone on bad mobile data is exactly the situation in which the worst case is the case.

**It is two populations against one ceiling, and that is the shape rather than a complication.** `MaxOccupants` and `SlopPopulation` are not derived independently: a слоп on the snapshot and a person on the snapshot are spending the same bytes, so the honest statement of the rule has two variables in it — **a viewer pays `SimHz × (solo + the peers he can see + the слопы he can see) + (the standings for everybody in the building) + (the events he is handed)` bytes a second, and the pair of populations is whichever combination keeps that under 8000 while leaving the game worth playing.** The second clause is not decoration: several combinations fit, and choosing between them is a design decision the arithmetic narrows rather than makes. As measured on 2026-08-03 — solo 180 B, the first peer +63 and each further +57, the first слоп +44 and each further +38, the standings 115 for one row and +87 for each further one, and events a flat 39 B/s — the whole grid of answers is short enough to state outright:

| 3 | 0 | 300 × 20 + 289 + 39 = 6328 | |
| 3 | 2 | 382 × 20 + 289 + 39 = **7968** | ← what ships, 32 B/s spare |

Three of those rows are what the calculator prints and the rest are the per-entity increments above, which is worth knowing before quoting one: the test measures what ships and the two states one place past it, so the interior rows are arithmetic rather than measurements. They agree exactly, which is the check that the increments are still the increments.

**Read the fourth row and then the fifth, because that pair is the whole finding: four people and ONE слоп do not fit** (8435 against 8000). The building was over the ceiling before the antagonist arrived at all, so this was never a case of trimming a field until something fitted. The слоп was first made the cheapest entity this wire can carry — 37 bytes against a peer's 56, dropping yaw (its facing *is* its direction of travel, which two consecutive frames give free), state (it never fires, never falls and is never protected) and health (one barrel is the whole of it) — and that was still not enough on its own. **The fourth place had to go**, and it paid for both слопы with 1227 B/s to spare for the kill column the standings grew. The alternatives were named and refused: a frame cap *below* the population — drawing only the nearest two of three — buys a place by creating a creature that can hurt you without being drawn, which is the one thing this game's visibility rules exist to forbid; raising the ceiling is not available, since it is the design's own trigger; and the binary codec is an iteration of its own whose case is stronger stated than half-done.

**Where to re-run it, because the answer moves whenever a frame does.** `TestEverythingAFullBuildingSendsAViewerFitsTheCeiling` (`message_test.go`) is both the gate and the calculator: it serialises `worstSnapshot(MaxOccupants, SlopPopulation)` and `worstStandings(MaxOccupants)` against `wireCeiling`, and then asserts that **one more person *and* one more слоп would each be over** — so it fails in every direction, and neither constant can drift above what the wire affords nor sit a place below it. `./dev.sh test -run TestEverythingAFullBuildingSendsAViewerFitsTheCeiling ./internal/gamevanyadum/ -v` logs the per-second cost and the headroom. **Take the digits from that run, not from this paragraph**, and write them into the grid beside `MaxOccupants` in `content.go`, which is where retuning any of the three frames has to pass.

**And one guard exists because the byte discipline has a failure mode that is silent.** The слоп array was first given the wire key `"z"`, which was already the eye height — and `encoding/json` emits **neither** of two fields sharing a tag, so that cut deleted the eye height *and* every слоп at once, with no error, no panic and no log line. Every player would have been drawn standing on the floor of sector zero and no слоп would ever have appeared; the only reason it was caught is that the byte count refused to move. One- and two-character keys are what this game's frames are made of, so collisions are a live hazard rather than a curiosity: `TestNoTwoFieldsOnAFrameShareAWireKey` now walks every wire struct in the package and refuses a duplicate.

**That instruction is written down because the digit has moved at every stage of this game's multiplayer work, and every move was a measurement arriving rather than a decision changing.** It was **sixteen** in the design doc, from an estimate of about 25 bytes for a peer; **four** the first time an entry was actually serialised, at 72 bytes worst case; **six**, briefly, when the shrunken 49-byte entry was divided into a ceiling that still counted only the snapshot; **five** once the standings frame was counted with it; **four** again once damage put a 7-byte state field back on every peer on every tick; and **three** now, with two слопы beside it. A record that stated only the answer would have been wrong at each of those stages without saying how to find out — which is why the formula, the grid and the command are above the digit here. The last move is the only one of the six that was **not** purely a measurement arriving: the arithmetic said four-and-none or three-and-two, and which of those is the better building is a judgement the numbers narrowed rather than made.

**The standings frame is inside that ceiling and not beside it**, which is the part worth stating as a rule rather than as arithmetic. It is traffic to the same phone, so leaving a second frame out of the sum would be moving the line rather than meeting it. The general form is that a budget only budgets what it is actually taken over, and this constant has already been wrong that way: **six** is what the same arithmetic gives when the standings are left out of the sum, and it lasted exactly as long as it took somebody to count them. The estimate era was the same failure one rung lower — the design doc's **sixteen** came of budgeting about 25 bytes for a peer against an entry that, once serialised, measured nearly three times that.

**And it happened a third time, smaller, inside this very record.** The **events** are the third term above, and they were not in the sum this record was first written from: the numbers here said 7929 B/s and 71 B/s spare where the calculator said 7968 and 32, because 39 B/s of pickup events were being sent and not counted. The digit survived — three and two is what fits either way — but *the headroom did not*, and the headroom is the number every future iteration is priced against. So the instruction is stronger than it looks: **the calculator is the source and this page is a copy**, and the copy has now been stale twice in one day.

**Interest management bought none of the raise, and the two changes must not be credited together.** A snapshot's peers are now filtered to the reader's own sector and the sectors joined to it by a portal — the sector graph already publishes that adjacency, so the filter is a table of booleans built once per building (`buildVisibility`, `World.canSee`) rather than a line-of-sight sweep per pair per tick — and leaving that set is **held** rather than instant (`visibleHold`, `World.heldVisible`), because a sector is derived from a position, so a man in a doorway changes rooms without walking and drags the whole visible set with him at the tick rate. A hold can only *add* to a filtered set, so it cannot exceed the unfiltered one the budget is taken on. It makes the **typical** frame much smaller, which is what a phone actually experiences and is the whole reason it is worth having. It buys no **capacity**, because the budget is taken on the worst case and the worst case is everybody standing in one room, where the filter removes nothing at all. [ADR-052](./ADR-052-the-netcode-is-built-multiplayer-complete.md) named it as the first thing to add once peers appeared and this record expected it to be the half that raised the number; it is the half that raised nothing. A capacity derived from the typical frame would be a capacity that fails the first time everybody crowds into one doorway. One thing the filter did **not** cover was named here as cheap to fix, and the fix has since been made: the pickup mask was of the whole building, so a bit clearing named a place and the next standings named whose bag grew — together they located somebody the reader was never sent. That was accepted only while nothing in this game could shoot, and the exemption expired with the обрез: the mask is now cut to the reader's visible sectors with the same `canSee` the peers get, minus the hold, because a bottle does not walk between rooms (`World.SnapshotFor`).

**The нейрослопы are filtered on exactly the same terms, and they buy no capacity either — for a sharper reason than the people do.** They ride the same `canSee` table with the same hold, so a слоп two rooms away is simply not on the frame. But a слоп *walks at the nearest man*, so they converge on somebody by construction: the filter helps most while they are spread out and least at precisely the moment they are all in the room with you, which is the moment the budget is taken on. A capacity derived from the typical frame would be a capacity that fails the first time the game gets interesting.

**The fifth place, for the one iteration it existed, was bought entirely by shrinking the peer**, which is this project's bytes-on-the-wire rule applied to a repeating frame ([ADR-037](./ADR-037-one-account-is-one-entity-and-the-wire.md) is the same rule applied to a face): what is constant for the life of an occupant is published once and addressed by an index afterwards. The 12-character pseudonym became a **slot** — a place in the building, small enough to be a digit, reused once its holder leaves, with the pairing published on the standings frame; the eye height became a **sector index**, which is smaller *and* more useful, since the client holds the level and can look the floor up in it; and the pose enum went because it was zero in every frame ever sent. The entry went from 71 bytes to 49, and that is the whole of it.

**And damage spent that place again, which is the derivation working rather than the decision moving.** Making a hit visible put `st` on every peer on every tick — 7 bytes, the floor for a JSON field, taking the entry from 49 back to 56 — and it is priced at the snapshot rate rather than at the trigger's cadence because most of its values are **durations** rather than instants: a man is down for `DownTime`, protected for `SpawnProtectSeconds` and rooted for `SyringeSeconds`, each true on every tick it lasts, with no honest duty cycle to discount by. Nothing cheaper was available, because being shot moves nobody, so it cannot be derived from a value the frame already carries. The self timers `dn` and `pr`, and the two counters the standings row grew (deaths and betrayals), are the rest of it. Four, and the trade went exactly as the constant's own doc block had predicted the next field of that size would go: a smaller building, never a larger ceiling.

**And the нейрослопы spent the fourth place, which is the same trade one rung further along.** The creature was made as cheap as this wire can express before any place was given up — 37 bytes, three fields fewer than a peer, each omission argued on the type (`message.go`, `Foe`) — and it still did not fit, because the building was already over the ceiling at four people with *one* of them. So `MaxOccupants` went to **three** and `SlopPopulation` to **two**, and the standings took +11 bytes a row for the kill column, which is not optional: a kill counter that is not published is a kill counter nobody has.

**32 B/s is a byte and a half of a snapshot, and that is the finding rather than the number.** JSON is exhausted here. There is no further byte worth trimming — a peer is six integers behind keys of one to three characters, a слоп is four, and the rest of both entries is punctuation — so **the next field of any size at all, on any of the three frames, costs the third occupant.** The JSON floor for a field is six bytes, which on a frame that repeats twenty times a second is 120 B/s: not a tight fit against 32, but nearly four times the whole of what is left. That binds every future iteration of this game — a new stat on the standings, a second counter on the bag, a kind byte on the слоп, an art key for one, all of them are now priced in *places in the building* rather than in bytes. Getting a place back, or a third слоп, needs the **binary codec** the design doc earmarked as an iteration of its own — not another round of trimming, and not a higher ceiling, which is a phone on RU mobile data and the design's own trigger.

**Which leaves exactly one move that is free, and the шприц is the worked example of it: give a field that is already being sent a second meaning.** An ampoule roots a man for a few seconds and heals him while it empties, so both halves of this project's visibility rule apply — he has to be told how long, and the other two people in the building have to see the most exploitable state in the game. Neither half bought a field. What the injector reads rides **`dn`**, the down timer, with **`hp` as the discriminator**: health at zero means the number is a respawn, health above it means the number is an ampoule. What the room sees rides a **fifth value of `Peer.St`**, a field already on every peer on every tick, where a fifth digit inside an integer that holds one value at a time costs nothing whatsoever. Two conditions make that legal rather than clever, and both are structural rather than conventional: the two meanings of `dn` are **exclusive by construction** — a dead man collects nothing and being hurt is what ends an injection, so no state carries both — and **another field already on the frame says which**, so no reader ever has to guess. Written the obvious way instead, `,"ij":2500` would have been 200 B/s against 32.

**The reuse has a limit and it is measured, not assumed.** A borrowed field is only free while the borrowed *width* is enough: `dn` is budgeted at `DownTime`'s digits, so an injection longer than that would add a character to a frame going out twenty times a second and would do it silently, since nothing else in the budget knows the field has two meanings. `TestTheAmpouleRidesTheDownTimerWithoutWideningIt` pins exactly that relationship. And the iteration paid one honest correction on the way in: `worstEventCost` had `"beer"`'s length hardcoded as the widest pickup key, which was true only while there was one kind, so it now derives the width from the catalogue — the wrong direction for a budget to be wrong in, because this one decides how many people fit in the building.

**And that finding did not become a record of its own, which is a judgement worth writing down because the next person to read it will ask.** The case for taking ADR-061 is not weak. The bar in `CLAUDE.md` is *the cost of a whole class of future change*, and "every wire field this game ever adds is now priced in places in the building, until the encoding changes" is that sentence almost word for word — it is exactly the kind of constraint somebody will otherwise rediscover by proposing a field and being told no. Three things decided against it anyway.

- **It is not a new decision. It is this record's own decision reaching its floor.** What ADR-060 settled is that *capacity is derived from what a viewer is sent rather than chosen* — and exhaustion is simply what that derivation returns once the encoding has no slack left. A record whose entire content is another record's arithmetic arriving at a particular value is a summary, not a decision, and it would have to be rewritten every time the value moved, which here has been six times in one day.
- **Splitting it would recreate exactly the failure this project abolished amendment chains to end.** The formula, the grid, the command that re-derives it, the six-value history of the digit, the alternatives refused, and the exhaustion are one argument; on two pages, the plain question — *what may I put on a frame?* — needs both of them read in the right order to answer. `CLAUDE.md` says a record states what we do about X and that following X across three files is the thing to avoid; the wire budget is one X.
- **It is not system-shaping, because it is not system-wide.** JSON is exhausted in **this game's 20 Hz snapshot at a full building** and nowhere else: the yard broadcasts at 5 Hz over a much smaller frame, and the office keeps its own budget and its own populations. [ADR-028](./ADR-028-games-are-self-contained-modules.md) puts a constraint that binds one game in that game's own record, and this one binds one game.

What **would** clear the bar is the **codec itself** — a change of encoding on a client-facing wire, with a decoder in the browser, a versioning story and a way to keep the golden vectors honest. That is a decision with a design behind it, and it takes its number when somebody makes it. Recording it now would be recording an *anticipation*, which is how a decision log fills up with things nobody has done; **020, 032, 035 and 036 are permanent gaps left by precisely that kind of over-recording**, and the numbering is deliberately expensive so that the temptation is felt. The place where the constraint has to be unmissable is the code, and it is: the doc block on `MaxOccupants` states it, and `TestEverythingAFullBuildingSendsAViewerFitsTheCeiling` fails the moment somebody adds the field anyway.

_An infinite match obliges the building to refill itself._ A заброшка whose contents are collected once and gone for ever is, a few minutes in, a building with nothing left to walk to. So a collected pickup **comes back where it stood**, after `PickupRespawn`, and the mechanism is chosen so that it costs nothing on the wire: a **deadline in ticks** indexed by position in the level's own list, which is the same key the snapshot's remaining-mask already uses, so a return is one bit changing rather than anything being re-sent. A tick rather than a countdown because an integer is exact where subtracting `dt` from a float accumulates the binary expansion of the step and no test could say which side of the boundary it landed on. And **there is no respawn event**: the mask is idempotent full state, so a bit going from clear to set *is* the announcement, and a client that wants to mark the moment diffs it against the previous frame — an event field would be bytes on a payload repeating twenty times a second, per viewer, to say nothing at all almost every time it was sent.

_What a visit is, and why the run could not simply be renamed._ A run had a beginning, an objective and a result: you started one over HTTP, you collected every beer, it ended, it was scored. A visit has none of those. The row records **which building** (`seed`), **when you walked in** (`joined_at`), **how long you actually had a connection** (`seconds`) and **how much beer** — and the length is measured to the last seen connection rather than to the moment the grace expired, because two minutes spent waiting for somebody who never came back are not two minutes they were here. There is **no `success` column and there never will be**: the field it replaced was `true` in every row ever written, which is another way of saying it recorded nothing. `game_vanyadum_runs` is therefore dropped in the same migration that creates `game_vanyadum_visits` rather than renamed, and the old rows are not translated — they describe a mode nobody can play, and putting them on a splash screen as visits would be a claim about a заброшка that cannot be walked into.

_What was given up, named rather than left to be discovered._

- **A seed-comparable leaderboard.** A run's row let two people argue about the same заброшка, which is the only thing that would have made a *durable* board worth looking at; there is no result to compare now, because how long you stay is a choice. What a match with no ending needs instead is something to look at in the middle, so the standings frame is a **live** readout of the building you are standing in — who is here, how long each of them has been, and what they are carrying — identical for every reader, unfiltered where a snapshot is not, and kept nowhere. `seed` is kept on the visit row anyway, because the question that survives — *were we in that one together* — is the only one anybody has ever asked of these rows, and it costs eight bytes.
- **An end condition, and the frame that announced it.** Collecting every beer ended a run; nothing ends now, so `vanyadum_over` and the result screen are deleted rather than left unreachable. What replaces an objective is the нейрослоп, and it has since arrived: the building keeps a population of them, they walk at the nearest man, one barrel is the whole of one, and killing them is the first thing here that scores. Two of the three slices between then and now changed what the building *costs* rather than what it *is* — the обрез gave the occupants each other to shoot at and spent the fifth place, the слопы gave them something worth shooting and spent the fourth — and the capacity above is where both differences landed. **The third, the шприц, gave them a way back from one barrel and cost nothing**, which is what a game rule looks like once the encoding has no slack left: everything it needed on the wire it borrowed.
- **Resume.** A reload used to reattach to a run that outlived its socket. It is now simply walking back in, and `AbandonGrace` is what keeps the place warm for the two minutes a tunnel, a lock screen or a refresh takes.
